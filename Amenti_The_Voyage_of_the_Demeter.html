One Cue Sheet · Three Books · The Ascent to the Castle

On the thirtieth of September a repository opened with one file in it: the first chapter, cut into cues. By the twenty-fifth of October two books stood finished, cast, recorded, scored, painted and bound, and the third had been painted down from Mina's branding to the rivers below the mountains. This account covers the voyage out, the hunt home, and the ascent that remains. It closes the record left open by The Demeter on the nineteenth.

Before the Studios

The bench in September held two other things: Moon Racer, the browser racing game run between real places on Earth, and the Ingram Manor page, the gateway to every project in the workshop. Behind both stood the work of the summer that the Studios would lean on most: the Library of Legends ledger of more than two thousand figures, each with a recorded manner of speech, and the speech engine that turns a figure and a line into a voice.

The Studios began as a question about that engine: if every figure in the library has a voice, can the library perform a book? The first answer was one cue sheet.

The stack

Every piece runs in a browser and is delivered without a terminal. The owner works from a 2014 iMac in Safari and the GitHub web editor; nothing in the chain asks for more.

The player loads in a fixed order: the speech bundle, then reader → casting → playbill → sound → art.

Phase III · The Book

The Demeter closed on an open question: illustrate a finished reading chapter by chapter, at an estimated hour or more of the owner's time per chapter, and two to three weeks of evenings for the rest of the novel. Five days later sixteen chapters are illustrated. What changed was not effort but shape: the novel was cut into books, and each book was finished whole before the next began.

Book IThe Castle · Chapters I–IVfinished 22 Oct

Jonathan Harker's journal, from Bistritz to the escape down the wall. About 400 paintings on the cues — no scene holds one picture much past thirty seconds — with 36 moving loops (lamp flicker, mist, water, clouds crossing the moon, stars), the Count's twelve stories told on a drawn map with a painting for each, the Peoples of Transylvania drawn on Jonathan's words, and the Count's letter written out as he reads it.

The page: a 52-second title film, chapters I–IV played straight through behind painted cards — wolves, the castle, the wall, the three women — and an ending with a credit roll. The cover: the castle under the moon, a gold cross centred on the moon, Darwin, Vlad, Hildegard and the three women on the back.

Book IILucy · Chapters V–XVIfinished 24 Oct

Letters, journals and diaries, from Whitby to the stake. About 470 paintings at roughly one a minute, in three sections: Whitby (V–VIII), The Blood (IX–XII), The Un-Dead (XIII–XVI). The cast's faces were painted first and used as references, so Mina, Lucy, Seward, Arthur, Quincey, Renfield and Van Helsing stay themselves across twelve chapters.

The colour follows the book: Whitby's last fine day in high colour, sea fog through the storm, Lucy in the gardens, then the long darkening. The owner's own giant-sun painting of the Demeter off Whitby opens Chapter VII. The moon in XIII–XVI is the real moon of September 1893, computed for each night. The page: a 57-second film, a painted card before every section and chapter, and an ending on Van Helsing's line — "And then begins our great quest." The cover: Lucy sleepwalking out of the garden gate under the moon.

What the books taught

Finish whole books
A book finished end to end — film, cards, ending, cover — is a product a reader can be handed. Sixteen half-illustrated chapters are not. The three-book split was Stoker's own; it also made the work showable.
Tag every batch
Each paste starts with a tag (BK2XII-A…). Midjourney's search then finds the set, shift-select takes it whole, and old pictures stop mixing with new. Six scenes or fewer per paste; a twelve-scene paste failed.
Upload generously, sort on the bench
Choosing in Midjourney's grid was the slowest step. Now the whole set goes to the Media Door and the sorting happens on contact sheets: black bars, faces, hands, story.
Faces before scenes
Reference portraits first, then every scene referenced to them. A character's look is locked once — Van Helsing clean-shaven, as Stoker has him, in alpine glasses and a leather coat — not argued again in every prompt.
Hands at full size
Hands are where paintings break down. All 620 placed paintings were reviewed once; since then every close-up is checked at full size before it is placed.
Check the empty boxes
A stray image left in the style-reference box tinted a whole book's pastes. The settings are checked before a batch, not after.
Density is a rule, not a feeling
Book I: no picture much past thirty seconds. Book II: about one a minute. A scene longer than a minute needs more than four pictures.
Never move a page a book points to
Printed books will carry QR codes into their chapters. The pages stay at their addresses; the print files live beside them in books/.

Where it goes next

Both books are finished but not finalized. Finalized means signed off and print-ready, and each book needs the same four things:

  2. An ISBN and barcode.
  3. A sharper upscale of the front-cover painting; at 1456 px it is soft for an eleven-inch cover.
  4. The digital stamps, once signed off: a QR code on each chapter opening into that chapter, and one Ask Amenti code for readers' questions about the story, the Count's history and Victorian London.

Beside it, five staged works — A Christmas Carol, Treasure Island, Romeo and Juliet, Quo Vadis, Julius Caesar — wait at their own stages, and each can follow the road Dracula has now surveyed.

Book III opens with Mina already taken. On the night of 2–3 October the Count comes for her; on the morning of the 3rd Van Helsing lays the Wafer on her forehead and it burns. From that hour she is marked, and the novel's last month is a race to lift the mark before the Count can claim her. Rev. A was written with that month unpainted. In the day that followed, the workshop painted it: from the branding on 3 October, through the escape at Piccadilly, the Thames, the Orient Express, the wait at Varna, Galatz and the rivers, to the rapids of the Bistritza on 4 November. The Chronometer

The idea was the owner's, and it came from the mark itself. Take the cross in a circle, the emblem on the covers and on Mina's forehead, and make it a calendar. The circle is the whole story, 360 degrees from the first entry to the last. The four points where the cross meets the circle are its cardinal points. The reader looks at the cross and follows the story around the rim. It was designed, built and published in one afternoon, between 4 and 6 p.m. on the 25th.

Two faces stand side by side under the title Chronometer, with a red cross-in-circle beneath it the size of the mark on Mina's forehead. The upright of the cross runs past the dial and on down to the foot of the page.

FaceWhat it showsBuilt from
ChaptersA 24-hour dial with the novel's 27 chapters set around the outside. Twenty-seven against twenty-four is nine against eight, so the two scales meet exactly three times, at I and midnight, X and eight o'clock, XIX and four in the afternoon, joined by a dashed triangle. Each chapter's arc is shaded day or night by the real sunrise and sunset at the place it is written from, with stars by night, a moon for each chapter, and the signs of the zodiac outside the rim where the sun changes sign.166 dated entries · NOAA sun
CalendarThe 187 days from 3 May to 6 November 1893 around the circle. The three books are drawn as bands, and Books I and II overlap, because Mina's and Lucy's letters begin on 9 May while Jonathan is still a prisoner in the castle. There is a tick for every dated entry and a mark for every full and new moon.3 May – 6 Nov

The Chronometer is not a picture hung beside the book; it is wired into it. A small emblem sits in the corner of every reading page and opens the dial over the paintings. As the reading plays, the hand follows the cue. Touch a date on the dial and the reading jumps to that entry, in this chapter or another. Its Map button opens the Dossier map for the current entry. Its Ask Amenti button carries the moment, chapter, date, place and document, into the Hall, so a reader's question begins where the reader is standing. Nothing is asked until the reader presses Enter.

The moon of 1893

Every moon on the dial and in the Book III paintings is computed: the moon that was actually in the sky on that night of 1893. Laid against Stoker's text, it tells a story of its own. From 3 May to 6 November the moon runs a little over six cycles of 29½ days.

1893The moonThe story
1 MayFullTwo days before Jonathan reaches Bistritz. On St George's Eve the moon, three days past full, "sailing through the black clouds", lights the Borgo Pass for the wolves.
16 MayNewStoker floods the castle with "soft yellow moonlight till it was almost as light as day" for the three women. The real sky was dark that night. His moon here is stage lighting.
12 AugNewWhitby, 11 August: "There was a bright full moon, with heavy black, driving clouds", the night Mina finds Lucy on the East Cliff. In 1893 the moon was a day from new. Stoker gives the scene the moon it needs.
26 SepFullFrom here Stoker and the sky agree. A waning moon gleams between the clouds over Lucy's tomb on the night of the 28th, and it is "so bright that through the thick yellow blind the room was light enough to see" on the night the Count comes for Mina.
10 OctNewThe hunters leave Charing Cross on the 12th under a dark moon, the London nights of XXIV–XXV moonless or nearly so.
25 OctFullVarna. The hunters watch the Black Sea for the Czarina Catherine. Stoker does not mention the moon; the paintings show it, rising over the water on its own date.
6 NovWaning crescentThe end. The thin moon has not yet risen at sunset. The last light is the red sun on the snow, and nothing else.

Phases are reckoned from the mean lunar cycle, accurate to within about a day, the same arithmetic the Chronometer uses.

So the moon draws the book's shape on its own. In May and August Stoker lights his scenes as a dramatist would, with a full moon whenever the horror needs one. From late September, when the hunt begins, the real sky and the novel move together, and the paintings follow the sky. When the owner was offered a full moon over the castle for the ending, he turned it down: no need to bend history.

What the hunt taught

Design the mark once
Mina's scar was argued over in mock-ups, in painted crops and in prompts, and kept coming back as a crosshair, a rubber stamp or wounds on her cheek. The answer was a portrait the owner chose, with the cross in a circle, red, exact. It became the face reference, and the words "the centre of her forehead, smooth clear cheeks" did the rest.
Colour can carry meaning
When Mina sees through the Count's eyes, the picture turns deep blue and her red mark is the only warm thing in it. After twenty chapters of lamplight, the owner called for daylight. Varna, Galatz and the rivers are painted under blue sky, and the night comes back only where Stoker brings it.
The sky is evidence
The moon in every scene is the moon of 1893: new on 10 October, full over the Black Sea on the 25th, a thin waning crescent by the end. Bending it for drama was offered and refused.
Turn the face away
When a face reference will not hold, as with the Count in a straw hat or Van Helsing's returning beard, paint the figure from behind. A silhouette is never the wrong person.
Count from the sheet
Cues are numbered from 1. Four chapters were placed counting from 0, so every picture arrived one line early. An end-of-day audit found it, and a single art.json moved 86 scenes back into place. Every package is now checked against the cue numbers before it ships.
Make the machinery forgive
A zip uploaded to the wrong folder had cost a round trip several times. Instead of asking the owner to be more careful, the inbox now gathers any amenti- zip from wherever it lands.
