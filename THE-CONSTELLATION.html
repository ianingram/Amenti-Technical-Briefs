The Constellation

The roster is not a list. It is the fixed grid the whole library is triangulated against — and it compounds.

A name that resolves to nothing falls through, unforced — the way a signal that hits no satellite is lost, not faked.
— the rule that keeps the bounce honest

The reframe

A resolver, not a register.

A historian’s text is unstructured — a stream of words. Among them are names: Tiberius, Ptolemy, Vespasian, hundreds more. On their own they are just strings. Bounced against the roster, each name either resolves to a known, dated point — or it does not. The ones that resolve become citable edges. The ones that do not fall through. The roster is the fixed constellation the corpus is triangulated against; the mention graph is the set of successful bounces.

The proof

Sixteen edges, and the six that were caught.

The first harvest — Josephus’s Antiquities, Books 14–19 — bounced against the roster and resolved 16 confirmed edges: Tiberius, Claudius, Augustus, Titus, Vespasian, Nero and their contemporaries, each citable to a book. And it produced 6 false bounces, every one caught:

  • Ptolemy — bounced off the wrong point: the text means Ptolemy Menneus, a local ruler, not the astronomer.
  • Suetonius, Livy — found only in the translator’s footnotes, not Josephus’s own words.
  • Brutus — the room-note excluded it: “this is LUCIUS… not Marcus.” The wrong Brutus.
  • Josephus — self-reference; an author is not an edge to himself.

A substring match is a candidate, never a link. The scan cannot decide; it can only present every signal a human needs to decide in seconds — count, date-fit, compound-name warning, room-note exclusion, first context. A whole book’s edges confirmed in about a minute.

The loop

It compounds. Most corpora decay.

This is the part that matters. The roster and the mention graph feed each other: a bigger constellation resolves more of the corpus, and the names that fail to resolve name exactly who is missing. The want-list writes itself.

The consequence

The corpus chooses the next cohort, not fame.

This inverts how roster growth is prioritised. The highest-value souls to add next are not the most famous — they are the ones the aboard texts keep naming but cannot resolve. Log the near-misses — a capitalised name, date-plausible, matching no point — and they are the next cohort, chosen by the library itself. The mention-graph harvest is also a want-list generator.

And it stays honest for the same reason everything here does: a bounce counts only if it resolves to a real dated point. No name is forced to land. A false resolution — the wrong Ptolemy — is a satellite locking the wrong beacon, and it is exactly the error the disambiguation exists to catch.

Build the constellation. Bounce the corpus off it. Let the two compound.
